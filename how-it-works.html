// How it works

What happens in the thirty seconds between asking and the verdict, step by step, using one real question.

// One question, start to finish

The question

Should I leave my job to work on my startup full time?

01

The gate

About 2 seconds

Before anyone argues, Council asks one thing: does this question have a single correct answer? If it does, you get that answer in one line and a nudge, because a debate would be theatre.

Leaving a job has no single right answer, so the panel is convened.

A fact

What is the capital of Australia?

Canberra. One line, no debate, and a reminder of what Council is for.

A judgment call

Should I leave my job to work on my startup full time?

No single right answer. The council convenes.

02

Three roles

About 15 to 25 seconds

Council sorts the question into a type and seats the three models best suited to argue it. This one is a career and life decision, so the panel is GPT-5.6 Sol, Claude Opus 5.5 and Gemini 3 Pro.

Each member gets one job and answers alone, in parallel, without seeing the others. That independence is the point: a model that can read the other answers starts agreeing with them.

The case for

GPT-5.6 Sol

You already have a working product and a few paying users. Full focus buys speed, and speed is what an early startup lacks most.

The case against

Claude Opus 5.5

Six months of savings is shorter than it sounds. First products usually need more than one change of direction, and each one costs time you would be paying for.

How to improve it

Gemini 3 Pro

Ask for a four-day week for three months. If revenue covers half your salary by then, leave. If not, you kept the job and learned why.

Illustrative answers, shortened.

03

The chairman

About 5 seconds

A fourth model, chosen from outside the panel, reads all three answers. It decides which seat made the strongest case for this person, names where the members disagree, and commits to one answer with a confidence level.

The verdict

Do not resign yet.

Strongest case: the case against. Where they disagree: how much runway is enough. The answer: move to a four-day week, set a revenue target for three months, and let that number make the call. Medium confidence.

04

Verification

On Plus

If the verdict leans on something checkable, like a statistic, a date or a rule, that claim is looked up in an outside source and marked supported, contradicted or not confirmed. Opinions cannot be checked, and Council says so instead of pretending.

Verification is switched off on the free preview so answers stay fast.

// The panel

The Advocate

The case for it. Argues the strongest honest version of yes, so the best case gets made properly rather than assumed.

The Critic

The case against it. Finds what breaks before you do. This is the seat most single-model answers are missing.

The Builder

How to make it better. Turns the argument into a next step instead of leaving you with two opposed opinions.

// Behind the verdict

The Chairman

Reads all three, picks the strongest reasoning and issues one verdict with the reasoning attached. It never sits on the panel it judges.

The fact gate

A question with one right answer gets a single line and an honest nudge. It stays silent when there is nothing to add.

Verification

Factual claims inside the verdict are checked against an outside source. Runs on the full version.

// Design decisions

Why three seats, not five

For, against and how to improve it covers the ground a decision needs. More voices add cost and noise, not new angles.

Why different models

A model asked to check itself mostly agrees with itself. Models trained by different labs have different blind spots, so one catches what another misses.

Why they answer alone

Members never see each other’s answers. If they could, the later ones would drift toward the first, and the disagreement you came for would disappear.

Why a separate chairman

The judge is chosen from outside the panel whenever possible, so no model ends up grading its own argument.

// What we hold to

Council principle

“It knows when to shut up.”

Ask something with one correct answer and you get one line, not a staged debate. You are not billed for theatre.

Council principle

“It refuses to fake certainty.”

The same confident tone for a settled fact and a coin flip is the problem Council exists to fix.

Council principle

“Every question has three sides.”

For it, against it, and how to make it better. The verdict comes with the split left in view.

// Common questions

What if all three agree?

Then the verdict says so. Three differently trained models landing in the same place is real evidence, and the confidence goes up with it.

Can I choose the models?

Council picks the panel from your question by default. Choosing your own three is part of Plus. The models page shows which panel each type of question gets.

How long does it take?

About 30 seconds for a full debate, around 10 for a quick take, and a few seconds for a fact.

Is this professional advice?

No. It is a structured second opinion. For legal, medical or financial decisions, use it to get clear on the trade-offs and on the questions to ask a professional.

Does Council remember my questions?

Not yet. Memory, so Council can draw on decisions you have already made, is the next thing on the roadmap.
