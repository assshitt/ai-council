// How it works

Inside a council session.

What happens in the thirty seconds between asking and the verdict, step by step, using one real question.

What is an LLM council?

An LLM council is a setup where several large language models from different AI labs answer the same question independently, and another model weighs their answers into one result. Instead of trusting one model’s view, you see where they agree, where they split, and why.

Think of it as a panel of advisers rather than one. Each model was trained on different data with different methods, so each has different strengths and blind spots. Council turns that difference into something useful: a verdict you can act on, with the disagreement left in view.

How Council does it

Council gives the models fixed jobs instead of letting them all answer the same way: one argues for, one argues against, one works out how to improve the idea. A chairman from a fourth lab then picks the strongest case and commits to an answer, with a confidence level and a receipt of exactly which models replied.

Blind spots surface

Different models catch different things. A risk one model glosses over is often the first thing another one names.

Fewer confident mistakes

When models trained by different labs agree, you can lean on the answer more. When they disagree, you know to check.

More than one angle

Each seat has a job, so you always get the case for, the case against and a way forward, not three versions of the same reply.

Confidence you can read

Every verdict says high, medium or low. A coin flip is labelled as one, instead of sounding as sure as a fact.

// One question, start to finish

Follow a question through Council.

The question

Should I leave my job to work on my startup full time?

01

The gate

About 2 seconds

Before anyone argues, Council asks one thing: does this question have a single correct answer? If it does, you get that answer in one line and a nudge, because a debate would be theatre.

Leaving a job has no single right answer, so the panel is convened.

A fact

What is the capital of Australia?

Canberra. One line, no debate, and a reminder of what Council is for.

A judgment call

Should I leave my job to work on my startup full time?

No single right answer. The council convenes.

02

Three roles

About 15 to 25 seconds

Each seat runs on a model from a different AI lab, so their blind spots differ. The case for is argued by GPT (OpenAI), the case against by Claude (Anthropic), and how to improve it by Gemini (Google). If one is busy, a free backup from another lab steps in.

Each member gets one job and answers alone, in parallel, without seeing the others. That independence is the point: a model that can read the other answers starts agreeing with them.

The case for

GPT (OpenAI)

You already have a working product and a few paying users. Full focus buys speed, and speed is what an early startup lacks most.

The case against

Claude (Anthropic)

Six months of savings is shorter than it sounds. First products usually need more than one change of direction, and each one costs time you would be paying for.

How to improve it

Gemini (Google)

Ask for a four-day week for three months. If revenue covers half your salary by then, leave. If not, you kept the job and learned why.

Illustrative answers, shortened.

03

The chairman

About 5 seconds

A fourth model from another lab, Qwen (Alibaba), reads all three answers. It decides which seat made the strongest case for this person, names where the members disagree, and commits to one answer with a confidence level.

The verdict

Do not resign yet.

Strongest case: the case against. Where they disagree: how much runway is enough. The answer: move to a four-day week, set a revenue target for three months, and let that number make the call. Medium confidence.

04

Verification

Planned for Plus

On Plus, if the verdict leans on something checkable, like a statistic, a date or a rule, that claim will be looked up in an outside source and marked supported, contradicted or not confirmed. Opinions cannot be checked, and Council will say so instead of pretending.

Verification is switched off on the free preview so answers stay fast.

// The panel

Every question has three sides.

The Advocate

The case for it. Argues the strongest honest version of yes, so the best case gets made properly rather than assumed.

The Critic

The case against it. Finds what breaks before you do. This is the seat most single-model answers are missing.

The Builder

How to make it better. Turns the argument into a next step instead of leaving you with two opposed opinions.

// Behind the verdict

What keeps it honest.

The Chairman

Reads all three, picks the strongest reasoning and issues one verdict with the reasoning attached. It runs on a different model from the three it judges.

The fact gate

A question with one right answer gets a single line and an honest nudge. It stays silent when there is nothing to add.

Verification

Planned for Plus: factual claims inside the verdict get checked against an outside source. Not live in the free preview yet.

// Design decisions

Why it is built this way.

Why three seats, not five

For, against and how to improve it covers the ground a decision needs. More voices add cost and noise, not new angles.

Why different models

A model asked to check itself mostly agrees with itself. Models trained by different labs have different blind spots, so one catches what another misses.

Why they answer alone

Members never see each other’s answers. If they could, the later ones would drift toward the first, and the disagreement you came for would disappear.

Why a separate chairman

The judge is chosen from outside the panel whenever possible, so no model ends up grading its own argument.

// What we hold to

We debate before we believe.

Council principle

“It knows when to shut up.”

Ask something with one correct answer and you get one line, not a staged debate. You are not billed for theatre.

Council principle

“It refuses to fake certainty.”

The same confident tone for a settled fact and a coin flip is the problem Council exists to fix.

Council principle

“Every question has three sides.”

For it, against it, and how to make it better. The verdict comes with the split left in view.

// Common questions

Before you ask.

What if all three agree?

Then the verdict says so. Three differently trained models landing in the same place is real evidence, and the confidence goes up with it.

Which AI models does Council use?

The main panel is GPT (OpenAI) for the case for, Claude (Anthropic) for the case against and Gemini (Google) for how to improve it, with Qwen (Alibaba) as chairman. GPT and Claude are paid models, so each day they answer up to a set budget; after that, or if a model is busy, free open models such as gpt-oss (OpenAI) and Llama (Meta) step in. Every verdict ends with a model receipt showing the exact model each provider says answered.

Can I choose the models?

Not yet. Choosing your own panel is planned for Plus. The models page shows the panels we plan to offer for each type of question.

How long does it take?

About 30 seconds for a full debate, around 10 for a quick take, and a few seconds for a fact.

Is this professional advice?

No. It is a structured second opinion. For legal, medical or financial decisions, use it to get clear on the trade-offs and on the questions to ask a professional.

Does Council remember my questions?

Not yet. Memory, so Council can draw on decisions you have already made, is the next thing on the roadmap.
